Perturbations and secular evolution

Real systems are not isolated conics. Additional bodies, source shape, rotation and dissipative coupling change orbital elements over many periods. Precession, nodal regression, apsidal advance, resonant locking and migration are therefore read as accumulated perturbations of the L17–L18 state, not as new primitive interactions.

Oblateness and higher multipoles require a resolved source geometry beyond a point koppa. Tidal dissipation requires a lag and internal-response model. Radiation or medium-resistance losses require a preregistered flux law. The point-source kernel does not silently contain those effects.

Planetary systems

Planets, regular moons, irregular captures, rings and small bodies occupy different dynamical regimes inside one source hierarchy. A credible calculation must state the inertial frame, all included sources, body radii, initial epoch, osculating state and excluded non-conservative effects. Stability claims require whole-range integrations and perturbation tests.

Retrograde bodies are especially diagnostic: the mean orbital seat and closest passage answer different questions. A proposed dragged layer must predict a quantitative boundary before the moon census is consulted; captured or migrating exceptions cannot be assigned after the result.

Stars, binaries and compact systems

Stars add pressure processing, nuclear construction, fluid circulation, rotation and mass loss to the orbital problem. Binary motion uses the same mutual-source equations, while eccentric systems provide a stringent test of any proposed resistance-radiation law. Compact boundaries require the finite-depth and c-boundary terms to be stated explicitly.

Stellar interiors and atmospheric outflows are extended-fluid problems coupled to celestial motion. They are not solved merely by fitting a point source to the exterior orbit.
