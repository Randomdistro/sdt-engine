## 1. The question that organises everything
Did relativity eliminate the medium, or did it move the medium's mechanical constraints into the definition of space, time and matter? The documented answer is: different people answered differently, and Einstein himself changed his answer between 1905 and 1920.

## 2. The people, one by one

### 2.1 Abraham (Göttingen, 1902–03; died 1922)
- **Documented [S]:** a rigid spherical electron with all of its inertia electromagnetic (a self-field reaction), giving separate longitudinal and transverse masses that diverge as v → c; he held to the rigid model and remained sceptical of relativity. Kaufmann's measurements (1901–06) were at first read as favouring Abraham over Lorentz; Bucherer (1908–09) found for the Lorentz–Einstein form.
- **On mass:** for Abraham the inertia is entirely the field's reaction; there is no mass of the body that is not the medium's. This is the strongest historical precedent for "mass is imparted".
- **Fit with the spation [I]:** the picture of resistance to acceleration as the medium's response is shared. Two real differences to resolve, not smooth: (a) Abraham's mass scales as the field energy over the radius, m ∝ e²/(Rc²): a smaller body is heavier. SDT's engine has mass ∝ the exclusion volume V_disp (Law IV), a larger volume heavier, together with a lock rule R = (W+1)ℏ/(mc) in which a smaller boundary radius goes with a larger mass. Which radius or volume carries the mass is a question the engine's two statements must answer together. (b) His rigid sphere cannot be stable or relativistic; the toroidal vortex of SDT has its own stability argument, which is what Poincaré's stress was invented to supply.
- **What he would ask:** the self-energy integral, the direction dependence of the resistance, and a stability condition for the particle.

### 2.2 Lorentz (1904)
- **Documented [T]:** the electron, a sphere in its rest state, has "its dimensions changed by the effect of a translation, the dimensions in the direction of motion becoming kl times"; the forces between the particles of a body are "influenced by a translation in quite the same way as the electric forces"; the masses are m₁ = (e²/6πc²R) d(klw)/dw and m₂ = (e²/6πc²R) kl; and "I shall suppose that there is no other, no 'true' or 'material' mass" (§9). In modern notation m₁ = γ³m₀, m₂ = γm₀.
- **A conflict between my sources:** one secondary summary [S] says Lorentz proposed both electromagnetic and "true" mechanical mass. §9 above is a supposition made for that argument; it may differ from his wider view. Not resolved here.
- **On the medium [V, Einstein 1920]:** Lorentz kept the ether and left it one mechanical property, immobility.
- **Fit [I]:** the closest historical match to the SDT premise that all inertia is the medium's response and that bodies are changed by moving through it. His electron needed a non-electromagnetic stress to hold together (Poincaré supplied it), which is the same gap SDT answers with vortex stability.
- **What he would ask:** the constitutive structure of the medium, and how a body with a finite size passes the medium on without absorbing it.

### 2.3 Poincaré (1900–1912)
- **Documented:** from *La valeur de la science* (1905) [S via search result]: "Peu nous importe que l'éther existe réellement, c'est l'affaire des métaphysiciens ; l'essentiel pour nous c'est que tout se passe comme s'il existait et que cette hypothèse est commode pour l'explication des phénomènes." Also, as quoted: "A day will undoubtedly come when the ether will be rejected as useless." He named the Lorentz transformations and showed that they form a group (1905–06 [B]); he introduced a stress to stabilise the deformable electron [S]; in his 1909 Göttingen lectures [S] he kept the ether and absolute time as unobservable in principle.
- **Fit [I]:** he would treat the spation as a convenience hypothesis and ask whether it is economical and testable. His own test is the one that matters: the medium's relay rules must give a relativity principle at the level of observation (no first-order detection of motion through it), and the particle needs a stability condition.
- **What he would ask:** Lorentz-covariance of the relay at the observable level, a stability condition, and the energy balance for a body moving through the convergent flow.

### 2.4 Einstein 1905
- **Documented [T]:** the ether "will prove to be superfluous inasmuch as the view here to be developed will not require an 'absolutely stationary space' provided with special properties"; postulate 2: "Any ray of light moves in the 'stationary' system of co-ordinates with the determined velocity c, whether the ray be emitted by a stationary or by a moving body"; §10 gives longitudinal and transverse masses and says "With a different definition of force and acceleration we should naturally obtain other values for the masses."
- **Reading [I]:** the argument is about the absence of a preferred state of rest that can be observed, not about the absence of anything physical in space. The limiting speed became a postulate about light and the coordinate systems; the medium's resistance to acceleration became the dynamics of a material point with a rest mass.

### 2.5 Minkowski (Cologne, 21 September 1908; died 1909)
- **Documented [T]:** "Henceforth, space for itself, and time for itself shall completely reduce to a mere shadow, and only some sort of union of the two shall preserve independence." On contraction: "For the contraction is not to be thought of as a consequence of resistances in the ether, but purely as a gift from above, as a condition accompanying the state of motion." And: "In this way even those, who find it unsympathetic or even painful to give up the old, time-honoured concepts, will be reconciled by idea of a pre-established harmony between pure mathematics and physics." He credits Einstein with seeing that "the time of an electron is as good as the time of any other electron".
- **This is the sentence the thesis points at:** Minkowski states outright that the contraction should not be thought of as a resistance in the ether, and that it comes "from above". That is the explicit refusal of the mechanical explanation which the spation proposes to restore. It is the documented place where a quality of the medium (resistance) was replaced by a property of the motion.
- **A correction to the pasted text:** Minkowski space is flat (pseudo-Euclidean); calling it pseudo-Riemannian is a loose use of the word. Curvature enters with Einstein's general theory.
- **Adoption [B]:** Minkowski's formulation was taken up by Sommerfeld, Laue, Born and others and became the standard arena within a few years; Einstein's own adoption of the geometric language came with the general theory. Not checked in this pass.

### 2.6 Einstein 1920 (Leiden)
Quoted in the companion notes [V]. In short: the stationary medium was dropped because an asymmetry in the theory with "no corresponding asymmetry in the system of experience" is "intolerable"; the special theory "does not compel us to deny ether" provided no definite state of motion is ascribed to it; Mach's idea of an ether that conditions inertia "finds its full development in the ether of the general theory of relativity"; "space without ether is unthinkable" because without it there would be "no possibility of existence for standards of space and time (measuring-rods and clocks)"; and the medium need not have trackable parts (the floats-on-water argument). He left open whether it "has an essential share in the structure of the electrical elementary particles constituting matter".

### 2.7 Weyl (1918–1920) [B]
- **Background, not checked here:** Weyl's 1918 extension of Riemannian geometry let the length standard vary from point to point (a gauge of scale), in an attempt to unify gravitation and electromagnetism. Einstein's objection was that the length of a clock's rate would then depend on its history, in conflict with the sharpness of atomic spectra.
- **Fit [I]:** the spation's local-state dependence of standards is the same kind of idea; Einstein's objection to Weyl is therefore the template for the test the spation must pass (the constancy of atomic and nuclear standards in different pressure regimes).

### 2.8 Mach
- **Documented [S]:** a critique of absolute space; inertia as a relation to the whole of matter; the phrase "Mach's principle" is Einstein's. The 1913 preface rejecting relativity (published in 1921) is disputed: one scholar argues it was written by Mach's son, and a letter of 1914 shows a favourable view of Einstein [S via a search result; not checked].
- **Einstein on Mach [V]:** the ether of Mach "not only conditions the behaviour of inert masses, but is also conditioned in its state by them".
- **Fit [I]:** SDT supplies the mechanism Mach lacked: inertial response as the displacement of the convergence by a body, and the convergence state conditioned by the bodies in turn (occlusion).

## 4. The thesis, stated so that it can be tested
**The statement:** "As an object approaches c, its mass approaches infinity, disallowing c: a clear indicator that mass is imparted, not a native property of matter."

**What the sources show:**
- The divergence was first derived (Abraham 1902–03, Lorentz 1904) as an effect of the medium on the particle: the particle's mass was the field's reaction. So the reading "imparted" is the original reading.
- In Einstein's kinematic version the divergence is in energy and momentum for a body of invariant rest mass (E = γmc²); in 1948 he advised against calling γm "the mass". The statement "if mass were native it would stay stable all the way to c" is true of the rest mass in the standard account: the rest mass does stay stable. What diverges there is the energy, the momentum, and the force needed per unit acceleration.
- So the divergence alone does not decide between the two readings; both reproduce it. The indicator becomes decisive only if the two readings differ in some other observable.

**The observables where they would differ [I], to register natively:**
1. **Direction dependence.** If the resistance is the medium's response to altering the phase of the convergence, the resistance to changing speed along the motion and across it comes from the relay geometry, with the known ratio γ² between the longitudinal and transverse masses (modern definition) as the target. The relay model must produce it with no spacetime postulate. Agreement is convergent; the discriminating part is any regime where the relay model departs (the c-boundary region).
2. **Dependence on the local state of the medium.** If the resistance is imparted by the local medium, the rest mass of a body (and the ratios of masses of different particles) could depend on the local pressure regime; in the standard account the rest mass is invariant. Tight limits on such dependence exist from comparisons of atomic clocks and tests of the equivalence principle [B, to be sourced], and are the place where Einstein's objection to Weyl bites. SDT's statement (mass per baryon constant, resistance depending on the state) has to be put in a form that either passes or fails them.
3. **Added mass.** A classical analogue exists in an ideal (inviscid, incompressible) fluid: a body moving at constant speed feels no drag (d'Alembert), yet it carries an added mass, for a sphere one half of the displaced fluid's mass, with a direction-dependent tensor for other shapes [B]. This is the closest classical statement of inertia imparted by an inviscid medium without dissipation, and it answers the drag objection of the Le Sage lineage in the way the pasted text half-states. In linearised compressible flow the factor 1/√(1 − M²) appears (the Prandtl–Glauert factor), with M = v/c_s [B]; whether the relay model produces the Lorentz factor as an exact result or only as an analogy is a derivation to carry out and check.

## 6. What each would probably have asked, in one line each [I]
- Abraham: "Where is the self-energy integral, and why is your particle stable?"
- Lorentz: "What is the constitutive law of the medium, and what does a finite body do to it that is not absorption?"
- Poincaré: "Is it economical, is it Lorentz-covariant where observed, and what experiment cannot be explained without it?"
- Einstein: "Does it give the full light deflection and the sharp spectra, and which of my properties of the ether do you keep: no parts tracked through time, no state of motion?"
- Minkowski: "Is the structure of the relay exactly my world-geometry for uniform motion?"
- Weyl: "What happens to a clock that is carried round a loop through different pressures?"
- Mach: "Show me the inertia of a body arising from the rest of the universe, with a number."
