Scene 1 — The unit cell of frustration

A single regular tetrahedron

The regular tetrahedron is the densest way four points can sit equidistant — the natural local cluster of a pressure-packed medium. Its dihedral angle (the angle between two faces along a shared edge, highlighted in copper) is the seed of everything that follows.

dihedral δ = arccos(1/3) = 70.528779°
bond / "methane" angle = arccos(−1/3) = 109.471°  (supplement of δ)

The bond angle 109.47° is forced: the four vertex-vectors from the centre sum to zero, so each pair must subtend exactly arccos(−1/3). Nothing here is chosen — it is pure geometry.

Scene 2 — Five around an edge

The gap that will not close

Try to wrap regular tetrahedra around a shared edge. Five fit — almost. Five times the dihedral is 5 × 70.5288° = 352.6439°, short of a full turn by an angular deficit that no amount of rotation can absorb:

Δ₅ = 2π − 5δ = 360° − 352.6439° = 7.356103°

That leftover wedge is the whole story. Regular tetrahedra cannot tile three-dimensional space. The medium that wants to pack as tetrahedra is geometrically forbidden from doing so periodically — it is frustrated.

Scene 3 — The Boerdijk–Coxeter helix

Face-sharing forever, closing never

If tetrahedra cannot ring an edge, they can still chain by sharing faces. Stack them and they spiral into the Boerdijk–Coxeter tetrahelix: each new cell twists by an angle that is an irrational fraction of a full turn, so the helix elongates indefinitely and never repeats.

twist θ = arccos(−2/3) = 131.810315° per cell
helix radius R = (3√3/10)·a = 0.519615·a
axial rise h = a/√10 = 0.316228·a

Because 131.81° is incommensurable with 360°, no integer number of cells ever brings a vertex back over its start. The chain is aperiodic by construction — the first hint that the vacuum is amorphous, not crystalline.

Scene 4 — Corner-sharing hinge

Compression is rotation, not squeeze

Once tetrahedra share only a single vertex instead of a face, the structure gains a soft mode: the two units can swing about the shared corner like a hinge, with no bond stretched and no tetrahedron deformed. This is a rigid-unit mode (RUM).

It matters because it tells you how the spation lattice responds to pressure: not by crushing the rigid tetrahedra, but by rotating them about their shared corners. The softness is geometric, and it is large.

SDT reading: compression = hinge rotation, not squeeze. This soft corner-sharing mode is the gravitational redshift in SDT — a region under load reorganises its hinge angles, changing the local closure length without compressing the units themselves.

Scene 5 — The 12-around-1 kissing shell

Twelve neighbours, and a gap

The most a single sphere can be kissed by, in 3D, is twelve. Place those twelve so their centres sit on a regular icosahedron — the most symmetric arrangement — and each kisses the centre cleanly. But the twelve cannot all kiss each other: the icosahedron's edge is longer than two radii.

icosa circumradius = 2r  (centres touch the core)
icosa edge = 8r/√(10+2√5) = 2.102924·r > 2r
surface gap between neighbours = 0.102924·r

So even the best shell is loose by 0.103r at every contact — the kissing shell is frustrated, the same frustration as the edge gap, now in spherical form. The shell's twelve radial modes decompose cleanly by symmetry:

12 = 1 (breathing, ℓ=0) + 3 (translation, ℓ=1) + 5 (quadrupole, ℓ=2) + 3 (icosahedral, ℓ=3)

Scene 6 — The corner-sharing random network

The only branch that can be space

Frustration forecloses the periodic crystal. The escape is to drop coordination: instead of face-sharing into helices or jamming twelve neighbours, each node bonds to just four others — coordination 4, the "W+1" of the SDT vortex grammar — sharing only corners. The result is a continuous random network (CRN): aperiodic, statistically isotropic, and genuinely space-filling.

coordination = 4  (corner-sharing tetrahedra)
diamond-limit packing fraction φ = π√3/16 = 0.340087
void fraction = 1 − φ ≈ 0.659913  (~66% open void)

This is the resolution. The amorphous, four-coordinated network is the only structure that is simultaneously isotropic (no preferred axis, so no broken Lorentz symmetry) and space-filling. A periodic FCC crystal would be space-filling but anisotropic; the icosahedral motif is isotropic but cannot tile. Only the random network is both.

Scene 7 — The screw channel

Current implies circulation

The void threading the network is not straight — the tetrahelix geometry makes every open channel a screw. A disturbance pushed along the axis is inseparably forced to circulate around it, because the channel's own screw partitions any motion into axial and circumferential parts in a fixed ratio:

axial fraction = 1/√10,   circumferential = 3/√10
torsion : translation = 3 : 1  →  energy = 9 : 1

This is a geometric mode ratio, not a coupling constant — nothing is tuned. But it has a physical consequence: an axial transport (a current) cannot exist without an attendant circulation (a magnetic twist). Current ⇒ magnetism falls straight out of the channel shape.

Scene 8 — The curvature reveal

The deficit is missing curvature

Where does the 7.356° go? It is real, and it is geometric: in a curved three-sphere (S³), five tetrahedra do close. That is the regular polytope {3,3,5}, the 600-cell, whose tetrahedra have dihedral exactly 72° — and 5 × 72° = 360°.

flat-space dihedral = 70.5288°
{3,3,5} 600-cell dihedral = 72°  →  5 × 72° = 360° (closes)
missing per edge = 72° − 70.5288° = 1.4712°  × 5 = 7.356°

The deficit is exactly the curvature flat space cannot supply: each of the five edges is short by 1.47° of the curvature that S³ provides for free. Flat space, lacking it, leaves the wedge open — and that open wedge is the frustration that forces the random network. The 600-cell shows the frustration is not a flaw; it is flat space's signature.
