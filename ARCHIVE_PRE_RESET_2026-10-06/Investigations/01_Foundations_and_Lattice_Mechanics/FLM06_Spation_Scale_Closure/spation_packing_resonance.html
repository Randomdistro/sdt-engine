1 · The two constructions

Both start from the spation's circular cross-section, cradled in the gap of three mutually-tangent circles. The only difference is how the next shell is placed — and that difference decides everything that follows.

A — ring · aligned (AAA)

Circumscribe the triad with a ring, then place the next three circles at the same angles (0°/120°/240°). Each new circle is tangent to the radially-aligned old one. Solving the tangency:

R(2−√3)=2+√3 ⟹ λA=(2+√3)/(2−√3)=(2+√3)²=7+4√3≈13.928

Everything stays in the number field ℚ(√3). Eclipsed columns.

B — valley · staggered (ABAB)

No ring. Rotate 180° so the next three drop into the valleys (60°/180°/300°), each tangent to its two flanking circles. That two-circle constraint is the twist — it injects a √2:

⁴⁄₃(R²−R+1)=(R+1)² ⟹ R²−10R+1=0 ⟹ λB=5+2√6=(√2+√3)²≈9.899

Lands in ℚ(√6) — a different field from A. This is the whole story.

3 · Do they ever recombine? — fields, near-misses & coprimes

An exact recombination needs λAⁿ = λBᵐ, i.e. the two ratios must be powers of one common unit. The derived pair are not: (2+√3)ⁿ always lives in ℚ(√3), (√2+√3)ᵐ never does (m≥1). So with the real geometry they never share a circle except the seed — but they come arbitrarily close, and the record near-misses are the continued-fraction convergents of θ = ln λA/ln λB, which are always coprime.

Try it yourself: change λA, λB below. Pick commensurate values (e.g. 8 and 2, or 9 and 3) and the verdict flips to exact recombination; pick the derived geometry (or any field-mismatched pair) and it's near-misses forever.
