Why a balloon expands, bursts, and how a cascade gets past it

A flexible envelope holds its gas at ≈ ambient pressure, so a rising parcel grows as the air thins. One cubic metre of sea-level helium becomes a small house by the mid-stratosphere — and that growth is exactly what tears a single latex balloon apart.

BWhy weather balloons pop

It is never the cold air "popping" it — the envelope runs out of stretch. Ambient pressure halves about every 5.5 km, so the skin keeps thinning until it reaches its tensile limit.
  • Expansion thins the skin. Volume up 100×+ means the latex membrane stretches and thins to a few microns; surface area can only grow so far.
  • Latex has a hard elongation limit. Natural-rubber latex ruptures near ~600–700 % strain — the diameter can grow roughly 5–7× before the material fails.
  • Cold embrittles it. At the tropopause (≈ −55 °C) the rubber stiffens and loses elasticity, so it reaches that limit at a smaller stretch.
  • UV and ozone attack it. Stratospheric ozone chemically cracks latex (ozone cracking), seeding the tear.
  • The tear runs in milliseconds. When the weakest, most-stretched spot exceeds the tensile limit, a rupture initiates and propagates around the envelope — the "pop". Typical burst: 28–38 km.
A latex sounding balloon's ceiling is set by its skin, not the sky — so it cannot dwell; it bursts and falls.

CThe cascade platform — staged expansion, not one burst

If a single envelope must survive the entire expansion it will always reach its limit. A cascade spreads the expansion across chambers that open in turn, so the system keeps lift at altitude without a catastrophic rupture.
  • Pinch-valve manifold. The primary envelope fills first; as it nears its elastic limit the falling ambient pressure prises the next valve open and helium floods the next chamber.
  • Float ceiling. h_float = H · ln( ρ₀V / m_total ) — each stage adds displaced volume V and lifts the ceiling.
  • Neutral-buoyancy stations. Each stage settles at its own float altitude, giving stable dwell points — the altitudes where the instrument's disks engage.
  • No single point of rupture. The expansion that bursts one balloon is distributed; spent chambers can vent rather than tear. Realistic terminus ≈ 50 km.
